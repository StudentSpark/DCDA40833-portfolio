    Visualization Critique

    Tableau Data Visualization

    Reflection & Analysis

    Data Preparation

    I decided to source my data from Kaggle, as it often has interesting and fairly straight forward data sets to work with. Once I found an interesting and well constructed dataset, getting it into Tableau was fairly straight forward - just drag and drop. The data was already well cleaned and organized, so it was very easy to use. However, it took some time to find data that was actually usable. For instance, the first data set that I thought was interesting was about housing in California - and I thought it would be interesting to see that geographically mapped. Unfortunately, the uploader of the data accidentally converted all of the longitudinal and latitudinal data into one average, so every data entry was at a single point in the middle of a desert, so not very helpful. Once I did find a suitable dataset, the only difficulty was downloading it off of Kaggle as its download feature doesn’t always work on my browser, but it was an easy enough fix.

    Design Choices

    I decided upon using a line chart to visualize the global life-expectency dataset I found. Covering 2000 to 2015, the visualization has a line representing every country on Earth (minus microstates, disputed territories, or North Korea) and its average life expectancy. After noting interesting data points, I highlighted certain reference countries to compare to Haiti and its notable outlier year. I also added an annotation to give further reference as to why the datapoint is such an outlier.

    Expected Patterns

    Based on my current knowledge and assumptions, I expected that most of the world would lightly trend upwards in life expectancy, with a few regions (such as Sub-Saharan Africa) potentially backsliding due to local economic and political instability. In general my assumptions were confirmed, with most places lightly trending upwards in life expectancy. There were some regions that did suffer from some life expectancy backsliding, but they were mainly on the Arabian Peninsula rather than Sub-Saharan Africa. These regions lost anywhere from 5-8 years of life expectancy.

    Surprises and Outliers

    By far the biggest surprise I encountered was the massive single-year dip of life expectancy in Haiti in 2010. After a small amount of research I realised that this was caused by the incredibly deadly 2010 Haiti Earthquake that devastated the country and killed at least 100,000 people. While the average person still lived as long, the number of people killed in roughly one day threw off the calculations.

    Questions for Further Investigation

    Besides the obvious dip from the Haiti Earthquake, I am interested in what could cause the longer term backsliding. I am guessing it is a result of wars or disease outbreaks that cause deaths over a longer period of time than a single incident. Another potential cause could be infrastructure collapse as a result of a disaster, war, or widespread lack of maintenance.

    Tool Evaluation

    I really enjoyed using Tableau as a visualization tool! It was great for fairly quick drag-and-drop applications and has a great variety of templates, charts, and map setups. My favorite parts are probably its ability to host interactable visualizations, allowing for viewers to click or hover over data points to give more context. Another visualization program I have a decent bit of experience in is ArcGIS, which specializes in geographic visualizations and general mapping uses. Its wide selection of basemaps, border / region management, and making printable maps for use in non-digital and non-interactive environments. That being said, the ease of use and wide range of visualizations (mainly non-map) of Tableau is very convenient and easy to use.
